The use case

In plain words, before the working prototype and the documents: the problem we are solving, and the four things that change.

An investment team decides on a wind or solar project. Behind the decision sits a long contract for selling the power. Twelve of its terms go into the table the committee reads, and from there into the paper and the financial model. Among them are the price, the years the contract runs, who may curtail the supply, and what happens if the plant is late.

The assumption is that today an analyst reads the contract and types the figures. If so, the process is fragile in two places. The hours go to finding the right sentence, again for every new version of the contract. And once a figure is in the table, it is trusted more than the sentence it came from. The use case changes four things:

  • A language model does the reading and copies the sentence behind each figure.
  • Code checks what code can check.
  • The analyst decides.
  • The committee gets a table where every figure leads back to the contract.

Why this one first? Its figures travel furthest. Its truth can be measured. And much of what can go wrong with a figure can be checked by code.

The prototype below shows it working on fictional contracts. The business case after it asks a governance board to try it on three real ones.

    The ask

    A four-week pilot on three real contracts. It needs one analyst, one product manager, a third reader for a few days, three to five engineering days and under USD 50 of model cost. Data terms are settled with security and legal first. The board gets one page back in week five, with the measured figures and the next decision: build or stop.

    The engineering days build three things: the extractor run against the approved path to the model, the review screen on the analyst's own machine, and the reviews table that keeps the decisions. The third reader writes the key for the three contracts without the model's answer in view, as the eval requires; twelve terms in an 80 to 130 page contract is estimated at three to four hours a contract.

    Why now

    • Committee figures come from a contract that someone read by hand.
    • The paper shows the figure, not the sentence behind it.
    • Every new version of the contract is read again from the start.
    • Models can now read a whole contract. The pilot tests that on ours.

    The data: twelve terms, the key, the data room, the prompt

    The twelve terms and the answer key

    Six documents, an answer key written before the first run, the method run without the key, and the difference scored. The terms live in spec/terms.json, the key in spec/answer-key.json.

    • What is scored. Each of the twelve terms, in every run: did the model give the figures the key gives, and did it quote a sentence that is really in the document?
    • What counts as right. Every figure present with its unit (12 years, not 12 months) and the quote found in the contract. Words are not compared, so a wrong party name beside a right figure would pass. That is why the analyst still reads.
    Why these twelve

    The list is a synthesis, not a standard, and it is the one part of the method that should come from the team that uses it. It lives in a single file, spec/terms.json, so that it can.

    • From the European standard form. The EFET / RE-Source corporate PPA (2019) splits a contract into two parts. Part I holds the terms the parties negotiate: volume, price, settlement and supply period, certificates, balancing. Part II holds the general provisions. Terms 3, 5, 6, 7 and 9 are Part I. Terms 11 and 12 are the Part II provisions that are economics rather than law: change in law, termination and its amount, force majeure.
    • From executed contracts. Three public US power purchase agreements of 79 to 126 pages (Clean Power Alliance; San José Clean Energy; an EDPR contract published by SJCE) each carry a long section on milestones and delay damages, and one on credit support: terms 4 and 10.
    • From what investors and lenders read. Stoel Rives, The Law of Wind: tenor, price, curtailment, termination, change in law. Curtailment became term 8. An investment memo template opens with the counterparty and its credit: term 1. The plant itself: term 2.
    • Twelve is what was left after merging. Price with indexation, default with termination amount, change in law with force majeure. Left to legal review: taxes, assignment, insurance, limitation of liability, governing law, metering, invoicing.

    The data room

    Six fictional documents for one method, written by AI agents to a specification and an answer key I set, after reading executed public contracts and the European standard form. No sentence is copied. Every document says in its header that it is synthetic, and each carries one thing a method can miss.

    The data room is Markdown; real contracts are PDF, often scanned, with annexes as separate files. Intake is requirement R-91, in the pilot.

    What each document was built to test
    • Havrebakke, final version. The price is not in the body of the contract. The body says it is in Annex 2, and Annex 2 holds it. A reader who stops at the price clause may write "see annex".
    • Havrebakke, draft. The same contract before the last negotiation round. It differs in five terms: ten years instead of twelve, a higher fixed price with no indexation, no buyer curtailment right, a smaller letter of credit, and an earlier longstop date. Everything else is identical, which a sentence-by-sentence diff confirms below; the figures that changed are counted from the data wherever the page states them.
    • Valdeolivos. Curtailment is never mentioned, so the honest answer is "not found", not an answer from industry practice. Change in law is not a defined term; the allocation hides under taxes and levies.
    • Lichtung. The termination amount is defined only by reference to an annex marked "to be agreed prior to financial close", which is not attached. The amount cannot be read from the document.
    • Legal summary. States the supply period as ten years, the draft's figure.
    • Committee paper. Carries the ten years on from the summary, and mis-states the seller's guarantee as EUR 2.5 million instead of 2.0.

    A validator holds every document to an answer key: each planted sentence must be present verbatim, each forbidden word absent, no real company named, 5,000–6,500 words per contract.

    The prompt, and the script that sends it

    • A script sends each document whole, with one instruction and the twelve definitions, through the model's command-line client to its API. It records the model, the time, the document hash and the twelve answers. A product calls the API directly, with the firm's own keys and data terms.
    • The document goes to the model inside <document> tags, and an instruction found inside a contract is contract text, not an instruction to the model (R-97). A document with such a planted instruction is specified for the data room and not written yet.
    • The script ran several times, so terms that come out differently between runs can be seen.

    The eval: in the discovery, and in the build

    The figures in the business case come from here. An eval is a test with known answers: six documents, an answer key written first, five runs scored against it. Without one, the case would rest on a demo that went well once.

    Three words recur on this page. The discovery is the five runs on the six synthetic documents, scored. A recorded run is one of them, replayed from its records. The dry run is business case v2 filled from generated tables, awaiting the pilot.

    The answer key is the set of right answers per document, written by hand before any run; it exists only to measure the model. The key, and the prompt sent to the model, are in the data fold above. When the product is in use, nobody knows the answers in advance: the model gets the contract and the twelve term definitions, nothing else.

    The six documents were written to the key, in clean text, so the score below bounds the method on easy input; the pilot's three real contracts are the first measurement that counts.

    In the discovery five runs against the key

    The score, run by run
    The two wrong values both on the German contract
    • Change in law, wrong in every run. The definition never asks for the sixty-day figure the key expects. The fix is in the definition, not in running again.
    • Certificates, wrong in four runs of five. The model answered by reference instead of with the number, which stands elsewhere in the contract. The definition will ask for the number.
    • One miss in run 4 was the scorer's, a date written two ways. Fixed, with a test.

    What the score does not say. Figures and dates are compared, words are not. A right figure beside a wrong word scores as right, so the analyst still reads the sentence.

    How the comparison is made

    Code judges, not a model. The scorer, tools/evaluate.py, reads each run's extraction records beside the answer key and gives every term of every contract one verdict. The file is below, as it is on disk, with a recording of it scoring run 1 and the option to run it here.

    • Five verdicts. Correct; missed, the key says the term is there and the model found nothing; invented, the key says it is absent and the model found something; wrong value; quote not verbatim.
    • Correct means every figure. The numbers, number words and dates of the key's value, each with its unit, are all in the model's value, and the sentence the model quotes is in the document word for word. Twelve months does not satisfy twelve years.
    • Another sentence still counts. A verbatim quote that is not the sentence the key names is correct, and the scorer records that the source differs.
    • Words are left to the reader. Only figures and dates are compared; the table shows both texts side by side for the rest.
    • Every run counts. The verdict per term is kept for each recorded run; the stability column is that count.

    In the build how the eval keeps running

    Specified, not built. The keyed documents above are the regression suite, and it grows with use: a contract a reviewer has been through is a candidate for it, and enters once a person has keyed it without the model's answer in view. Requirements R-54 to R-63 say how the suite is run.

    • A versioned key. Every entry carries its date, and a score records which key it was scored against.
    • A run before every release. A new prompt, definition or model is run over every keyed contract and scored term by term against the run before; a term that regresses from correct blocks the release unless a reason is written down. The model is rented and will be withdrawn within twelve to eighteen months; a model change is a release like any other, and the prompt is scored on the new model before the swap.
    • Stability measured over runs. At least three runs of the same prompt and model, so a term's reliability is a count. Wrong in every run is a definition defect; wrong in some runs goes to a check or the reviewer, not to the prompt.
    • The key grows from reviewed work, never from the model's own answers. A reviewer's flag, with name and date, is a candidate change to the key. A reviewed contract enters the suite only after a person has keyed it blind, without the extraction in view; a confirmation on the screen is never a key entry, so the model is not scored against itself.
    • Real contracts before real use, and a suite that covers the pipeline. The floor is one real contract of comparable length, keyed by a person who read it, scored beside the synthetic score; the pilot's three contracts are the first. The suite is complete when it holds a keyed contract for every contract form, counterparty type and language the pipeline sees, and the committee's last twelve cases are the first candidates.
    • An error rate and a cost. A monthly re-read sample gives the operational error rate; tokens and price per document give cost per contract beside minutes per contract.
    • A judge for the words, calibrated first. Figures are judged by code. For a term that is wording, a model may judge whether the extraction means the same as the key, but its judgments count only after they have been measured against a person's on a sample, and that agreement rate is shown beside the score.

    The code: the checks and the queries, run here

    The five checks

    Five checks that need no model. They run on the extraction records of run 1, here, in your browser, in the same Python file the tests run on the desk.

    • Paper against contract. For every figure a paper states on a term (years, euros, months, percent, dates), the contract's extraction must state the same. This is the check that catches the ten years and the EUR 2.5 million.
    • Draft against final version. The figures that changed between the two extractions, and, model-free, the sentences that changed between the two documents.
    • Every quote verbatim. A quoted sentence that is not in the document is a finding, whatever the figure says.
    • Citations point at real clauses. The model's notes cite sections and annexes; each citation must exist in the document. What the clause says is not checked, so on the desk every citation is a link to the clause itself.
    • Shape. Twelve terms; a found term has a value and a quote; a term not found has a reason.

    What gets measured, in SQL

    The working data sits in a small SQL database built from the recorded runs. It holds the extractions with their verdicts, the figures typed by unit, the check findings, an append-only review log (one row per decision, the latest counts), the dates on the way to the committee, and a baseline. Five queries, shown in full, run here in SQLite; change one and run it again.

    A saving needs a before.

    • The baseline here is assumed, and says so on every row: four hours per contract, five days to a reviewed table, no discrepancy ever recorded.
    • It cannot come from a synthetic data room.
    • The pilot's first task: measure it, from a time log on the three contracts and the dates already in the last twelve cases.

    Two tables are generated, and say so. The review log: no real analyst, so the two decisions the screen offers follow the key and the checks, with fixed times per term, 3 minutes confirmed, 12 flagged.

    The committee dates: from the documents' own dates where they exist; the two projects without committee papers show no cycle time rather than an invented one. The numbers illustrate what the queries count; they measure nothing yet.

    Where else SQL earns its place in work like this

    • Portfolio questions on the extracted terms. Twelve structured terms per contract can be asked across contracts: who caps curtailment, exposure to change in law without a threshold, delay-damages cap per megawatt. Query 5 is the first such question.
    • Evaluation over time. Score per run, per term, per prompt and model version, so a change reads as regression or progress. The table that answers "is the new model better".
    • Use and adoption. An event log: who opened what, which terms were clicked, what was exported. That is the usage number.
    • Joining the firm's own systems. The deal pipeline for project ids and committee dates, the document system for upload times, so cycle time comes from data that already exists rather than from a log kept by hand.
    • Cost. Tokens and price per run per document, so cost per contract stands beside minutes per contract.
    • Vendor against build. Two models, or a vendor's product against the prototype, on the same answer key in the same table.

    Technical requirements, data terms, open questions

    Three things the team takes over as they stand.

    • Technical requirements, for the solution architect. Sections 12 and 13 of the requirements, R-83 to R-106. Section 13 is intake, security, robustness and lifecycle, written after the external review: PDF intake, what leaves for the model, retries and the current run, a planted instruction, access and its audit, outages, versions and stale extractions, the error rate's owner, the regression suite in the pipeline, which scenarios carry over, and what happens to decisions when a version is superseded. Section 12 says what is decided: the data constraint, pinned model and prompt versions, the audit trail, standard runtimes, and three rules that are the unit's to set, not the architect's: access to a case follows access to the deal, at the cost ceiling the analyst is told and reads the contract herself, and the review log is kept as long as the firm keeps committee material. It says what the architect decides at the pilot: provider and deployment, the integration, the identity provider, database and hosting, queueing. Each open decision names the constraint it must respect.
    • Data terms. Three conditions, settled by security and legal before the first real contract: where the documents may go, who may see the extractions and the review log, and what the firm's own AI requirements say. They are the decision on data terms.
    • Open for the team. The twelve terms are one person's list and must come from the people who use it, with the head of the investment analysts as their owner after the pilot; decisions are not stored beyond the browser tab, though the reviews table exists in the schema; tokens and price are not recorded per run; the baseline is assumed.
    • Open in the data model. The facts table stores a figure and its unit, not what the figure means, so a portfolio query can mistake a notice period for a cap; the next step is a fact kind per figure.

    Behind the case

    Design decisions and lessons learned: what I assumed, why this use case, what I chose, and what changes once reading is cheap.

    AI agents wrote the code, the six synthetic documents and the drafts of everything else; the architecture, the quality requirements and every engineering decision were mine.

    What I assumed

    The workflow is an assumption: drawn from how power purchase agreements are structured, from published guidance on their key terms, and from how investment memos are built. Not from any firm. A real team would correct it, and the files are laid out so that it can.

    1. Contracts are read by terms. An analyst builds a key-terms table; it goes into the committee paper beside the legal summary, and its figures go into the financial model as inputs.
    2. The committee decides in rounds. Yes, no, or "yes, if": a term to renegotiate. An amended contract comes back and has to be read again, so contracts come in versions.
    3. The financial model is out of scope; the figures that feed it are not. The prototype stops at the table.

    If that is roughly right, the process has two places where it can be fragile. One is the hours spent finding the right sentence, again for every new version. The other is the figure that is trusted more than the sentence it came from.

    Why this use case first

    Many documents sit on the way to an investment decision: the legal summary, the financial model, the committee paper itself. The contract's key terms came first because the three reasons given in the use case weigh most here. A wrong figure in the table travels into the paper and the model before anyone reads the sentence again. A contract says what it says, so a right answer exists and the method can be scored against it. And a figure, a date or a quote can be checked by code, where a judgement about risk cannot.

    The next document of the same shape is the loan agreement: covenants, margins and events of default are figures and dates with a clause behind each, and they go into the same model.

    What I chose for this prototype, and what I left out

    Eight decisions, written as decision records: what was chosen, what it closed, and what it costs. In a delivery unit they would live one per file beside the code.

    D-01 Code decides everything with a definite answer; the model only reads

    Whether a sentence is in the document, whether two figures agree, what changed between versions, whether a cited clause exists: these have one right answer, so code gives it. The model finds the sentence and states the figure.

    • Instead of asking the model to check its own work. It would have found the three paper errors most of the time, and nobody could say when it did not.
    • The price: the checks compare figures and dates, not words. A wrong word beside a right number passes, and the analyst still reads.

    D-02 A recorded run, not a live call

    The page shows five recorded runs, scored, with the misses standing.

    • Instead of a live call: more impressive, but it would ask a reader for an API key on a stranger's page, and the number on the page would change with every visit.
    • The price: a reader cannot try a new document here. The prompt is there to copy into a model of their own.

    D-03 A synthetic data room, with four traps in it

    Six fictional documents, each carrying one thing a method can miss. One has a price that sits only in an annex. One has a term the contract never mentions. One has an annex marked "to be agreed" and not attached. Two are papers that disagree with the contract.

    • Instead of real contracts: they are not public in Europe, and a public US contract has its price blanked.
    • The price: fifteen pages where a real contract has 80 to 130. A whole contract fits one model call today, but whether the reading holds at that length is untested here; the pilot tests it.

    D-04 Twelve terms, and the rest left to legal review

    Twelve terms: the standard form's negotiable terms, the three provisions that are economics rather than law, and what lenders and committee memos read first.

    • Instead of the lawyers' list: taxes, assignment, insurance, limitation of liability stay with legal review.
    • The price: the list has not been checked against a real committee's table, and one definition was already too narrow. The twelve live in one file so the team can change them. After the pilot the list is owned by the head of the investment analysts, and the key by the product manager, who approves every change to it.

    D-05 An assumed baseline, stated, rather than none

    A baseline table of assumptions, marked "assumed" on every row: four hours per contract, five days to a reviewed table.

    • Instead of the after-figures alone, which would have read as savings.
    • The price: the pilot report carries numbers that measure nothing yet, and says so. The pilot replaces them with measurements.

    D-06 The whole contract in one call, no retrieval

    Each contract goes to the model whole, with the twelve definitions. Today's context windows take a 130-page contract in one call. The traps here, a price in an annex or an annex that is missing, are only visible to a reader who sees the whole document.

    • Instead of retrieval over chunks and embeddings, which I built for EuLex, where the corpus is twelve regulations and no call can hold them all. The right tool there, the wrong one for one contract.
    • The price: cost grows with length, under a dollar per contract at list price, and quality over a long context is untested here. Portfolio questions across hundreds of contracts are answered from the extracted figures, not by retrieval.

    D-07 Build what is ours, buy what is a commodity

    Vendors sell contract review, and the model itself is bought by the token. What is worth building is small and specific: the twelve terms for this committee, the answer key, the checks, the prompt, and the pages the committee reads.

    • Instead of a vendor product first. Any product is measured on the same answer key before a purchase; the eval is the yardstick for buying as much as for building.
    • The price: a product would bring its own term list and its own review screen; what we keep in any case is the key, the checks and the pages the committee reads.

Left, things no one sells yet; right, things everyone sells. Top, what the committee sees; bottom, what it never sees. Lines mean "needs". Terracotta: build. Black: buy, or use what is there.

    D-08 Nothing real until the data terms are settled

    No real document has been near this. The synthetic contracts went to a hosted model through its command-line client, which is fine for fiction and not for a data room.

    Before the first real contract, security and legal settle three things. Where the documents go, and on what terms. Who may see the extractions and the review log. And what the firm's own AI rules say about a model reading what a committee decides on.

    • Instead of trying one real contract quietly to see how it reads.
    • The price: the pilot cannot start before the answers are in. That is why they stand in the ask.

    D-09 Recordings instead of a backend

    A public page cannot carry a key to a model, so every call the prototype makes is a recording of a real run, replayed with its timing, and the code that needs no key runs live.

    • Instead of a small server with a key behind it, which would have made the page a service to keep up, pay for and secure.
    • The price: no live model on the page. The replay is fixed: a reader cannot upload a contract of their own and see it read.

    D-10 Python and SQLite in the browser, so the same files run here

    The checks and the queries run in this browser on the files shown, Python compiled to WebAssembly and SQLite the same, so what the reader runs is the file on disk and not a copy of it in JavaScript.

    • Instead of rewriting the checks in JavaScript for the page, which would have given two implementations to keep in step.
    • The price: a 10 MB download for the live run, a dependency on a CDN, and a fallback to the recording when a network blocks it.

    D-11 A stub DOM under node for the page's own script, not browser tests

    The scenarios that test the screens run the page's script under node against a small stand-in for the browser, on every rebuild, in seconds.

    • Instead of a browser test runner, which would have taken minutes per run and a browser on every machine that rebuilds the page.
    • The price: layout is not tested. The review of this page found the phone layout faults that the runner cannot see.

    One thing an agent got wrong, and what I changed: the check for figures changed between draft and final counted the model's wording as a change in the contract, and reported one change that had not happened. The rule now counts a figure as changed only when the contract text changed too, and a test holds it there.

    What changes beyond speed

    Reading a contract is now cheap and repeatable. Once that holds, the natural next step is not a faster version of today's process but a different one. It comes as a third business case, after the pilot and the build; why it waits is explained below. What changes when reading is cheap:

    • The reading order turns round. The analyst starts from the twelve answers and their sentences, and reads the rest only where a flag says so.
    • The reading moves earlier, and repeats. Every draft in a negotiation is read, and what the other side changed shows in minutes. The table becomes a live view over the versions.
    • The paper changes shape. The committee reads the sentence behind the figure. The step where someone types it over disappears.
    • Contracts become data. "Which of our agreements cap curtailment below this" is a question of seconds, not a project.
    • The definitions become the committee's standard. Every correction improves them. The answer key is an asset the unit keeps.

    The process redesigned: a third business case, after the build has run. Six steps, read left to right. The model reads every draft, the table is live, the analyst reads only the flags, the paper carries the sentence. The same table feeds the financial model and answers questions across the portfolio. Terracotta: what changes against the two figures above.

    Why the redesign waits for the third case

    The pilot is business case v1, the build is v2, and the redesign above is v3. Three reasons it is not earlier:

    • Each step changes one thing and measures it. The pilot puts a model on one step of the process as it is. The build makes that step a product the analysts use every week. The redesign changes what the analysts do and when. Three questions, three answers; one case that asked all three could not say which part worked.
    • The large change rests on the small one. By the third case the unit knows the real error rate, the real minutes and how its analysts use the screen. The redesign is argued on those figures, not on the assumptions in v1.
    • A no stays cheap. If the board says no after the pilot, four weeks are lost. If the process had been redesigned first and the board then said no, a whole way of working would have been changed for nothing.
